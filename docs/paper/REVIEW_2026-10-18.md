## 2. Must fix before submission

### 2.1 Three vs four models (Highlights ↔ Abstract)

Highlight 1 reads "One declarative contract runs **three** AI models"; the Abstract, Methods
and Table 4 all say **four** (Gemma 4 E4B, SAM 2, SAM 3, TotalSegmentator). The likely cause
is Table 1, which collapses SAM 2 / SAM 3 into one column, and the title's "three execution
models". Pick one count and make the Highlight say "four AI models under three execution
models".

### 2.2 GB / GiB inconsistency in the headline ratio

§3.3 and Table 3 read "declared 3.5 GB; measured single-process peak 20.9–23.1 GB
(19.4–21.5 GiB), **5.5–6.1×** the declaration". The ratio is computed in GiB
(19.4 ÷ 3.5 = 5.54; 21.5 ÷ 3.5 = 6.14) because the arbiter reads `ram_gb` as GiB. A reader
dividing the GB figures by 3.5 gets **6.0–6.6×**, and `exp3_FINDINGS.md:41` independently
states **6.5×**. Three different multipliers for the same defect is the kind of thing a
reviewer screenshots.

Fix: at first use, state once that `ram_gb` is interpreted as GiB and that all declared-vs-
measured ratios are therefore computed in GiB — then keep every ratio in that unit. Note that
the SAM 2 ratio in the same paragraph ("3.0 GB → 1.45 GB, 0.48×") *is* computed in GB; in GiB
it is 0.45×. Same subsection, two conventions.

Also reconcile the Highlight ("6× too low") and Abstract ("5.5–6.1×") with whatever you choose.

### 2.3 Two of the four models were never executed

Table 4 is honest about it, but the paper's central claim — a contract that hosts four
heterogeneous models — rests on **inference evidence for only two of them**. SAM 3 (gated
weights) and Gemma 4 E4B (no MLX runtime in the August campaign) have protocol conformance
only. Worse for the thesis: §3.3 concedes their memory peaks were never measured, while the
paper's conclusion is that hosts must *audit declarations against measurement*. Table 1
therefore presents two unaudited declarations (5.0 and 6.0) in a paper arguing declarations
cannot be trusted.

E6/E7 close this. Until they run, this is the paper's weakest point and the most likely
reviewer objection. Make sure E6 records peak memory for Gemma 4 E4B and SAM 3, not just
latency — that turns a limitation into a second data point for the headline argument.

### 2.4 Unfilled front and back matter

17 `CONFIRM` markers, including every item a desk editor checks before review: author list,
affiliations, ORCID, corresponding author, CRediT statement, competing-interest declaration,
funding, generative-AI-use declaration, ethics wording. The draft lists two authors
(D. Kim, J. Heo) against ten on the host-viewer paper [8] — settle this early, it affects
the cover letter's framing of overlap with the prior publication.

## 4. Target journal

### 4.1 Fit

CMPB's scope is explicitly software systems — "computing methodology and software systems
derived from computing science for implementation in all aspects of biomedical research and
medical practice" — so a plugin architecture for a DICOM viewer is in scope on paper, and the
journal is Q1 (SJR 1.233 for 2025).

The risk is not scope, it is the paper's shape. CMPB's commonly cited rejection reasons are
insufficient novelty and weak experimental or clinical validation, and this manuscript
deliberately makes **no accuracy claim** and contributes an architecture rather than a method.
Reviewers drawn from CMPB's current pool (the recent issue is dominated by deep-learning
imaging methods) may read "we integrated existing models and measured our own plumbing" as
engineering rather than research.

Two things reduce that risk, and both are already latent in the draft:

- Lead with the **generalisable finding**, not the artefact: an arbiter that is provably
  correct against its specification is still unsafe when the specification's inputs are
  self-declared. That is a transferable result about resource contracts, and it is the one
  sentence in the Conclusions worth promoting into the title and Abstract's first line.
- Make E6/E7 **quantitative**. Two complete experiments with latency and coordinate-mapping
  numbers across four engines and two machines give the paper a measurement core; without
  them it reads as a system description with a defect list.

Also, §4.2's argument that systems papers should report defects found by evaluation is good
and unusual — but it reads as a methodological opinion inside a software paper. Keep it, keep
it short, and do not let it become the paper's stated contribution #3; "we found bugs in our
own system" is easy for a hostile reviewer to recast as immaturity rather than rigour. Frame
it as *evaluation design* — which classes of defect only boundary-crossing tests reveal.

### 4.3 On the fallback cascade

`PLAN.md` lists JIIM → SoftwareX. One correction and one suggestion:

- **SoftwareX is open access with an APC and a ~3,000-word Original Software Publication
  format.** It would mean discarding most of the evaluation narrative and paying a fee —
  it does not satisfy the stated "fast, low-cost" priority. Treat it as a last resort, not
  the second fallback.
- **CMPB Update is not a soft landing from CMPB.** It is editorially independent with its
  own team and an open-access APC; a CMPB rejection does not transfer favourably.
- **JIIM is the stronger fallback and arguably a legitimate first choice.** It published the
  host viewer [8], its readership is exactly the DICOM-interoperability audience that cares
  about SEG conformance and coded anatomy, and a follow-up is a natural fit. The cost is
  lower prestige than CMPB Q1.

A reasonable decision rule: submit to CMPB **only after E6/E7 are complete and the Discussion
leads with the auditing result**; if either is still missing when you want to submit, go to
JIIM first, where the contribution needs less translation.
